Lateral Bets — Hot Takes and Open Questions

Most Shanepedia lanes are tracked separately: vending, Bitcoin, OpenCluck, OpenClaw, El Salvador, content. The interesting bets live in the crossovers — where two or more lanes collide in a non-obvious way.

This page is the running list. Each entry: why it is interesting, the first proof step, the biggest risk. None of these are committed projects — the point is keeping the speculation legible so it can be picked up when a lane ships a milestone that makes a bet ready.

1. Lightning egg vending machine

The bet: A small refrigerated vending machine at the farmland roadside (or a cooperating local business) selling dozens of eggs from Shane's flock. Lightning + card. Restocked twice weekly from the coop.

Why interesting: Tightest crossover available — [[../entities/opencluck.md]] produces the inventory, [[hot-sauce-vending-machine.md]] provides the vending playbook, [[bitcoin-circular-economy-portland.md]] provides the Lightning side. Cleanest content artifact possible: "I sold an egg my chicken laid for sats."

First proof step: A single cooler-with-padlock honor box at the farmland gate with a printed BTCPay invoice QR per dozen. No machine. Validate demand before spending on cold-vending hardware.

Biggest risk: Oregon ODA cottage food and farm-direct sale rules. Honor-box version is fine; refrigerated vending crosses into more regulated territory. Verify before any machine deployment.

2. Short YouTube series documenting the OpenCluck build

The bet: A 6–10 episode series, 4–8 minutes each, documenting OpenCluck from BOM to first deployed unit. Released as the build happens, not after.

Why interesting: The intersection of "ESP32 builds" + "backyard chickens" + "self-hosted IoT" is small but loyal and underserved — chicken content is suburban-cute, ESP32 content is generic-LED. Almost no competition at the cross-section. Also accelerates open-sourcing the firmware under [[../entities/cash-for-clankers.md]].

First proof step: Record a single 5-minute episode 0 with the BOM on a table. Post it without optimizing. See what comments arrive.

Biggest risk: Video production overhead. Shane's edge is text, not video. If episode 0 takes 20 hours, the series won't survive a busy week. The episode-0 test measures that overhead before committing.

3. Agentic Yelp for Bitcoin merchants

The bet: A site that takes BTCMap data as a base, layers agent-driven verification (calls/messages the merchant, asks "do you actually accept Lightning?", logs the answer), and surfaces a freshness score. Updated weekly. Portland-only first.

Why interesting: BTCMap is great as a community map but everyone who uses it knows the data is stale. A verification layer is the single most useful upgrade. Perfect agent task: structured input, structured output, runs on a cron. Crosses the OpenClaw stack with the circular-economy research lane.

First proof step: Manually verify the 30 BTCMap-listed Portland merchants this week. Publish a single page with the results. If the answer is "12 of 30 still really accept Bitcoin," that itself is a story worth shipping.

Biggest risk: Merchants do not love being cold-called by an agent. The bet only works if the verification is polite and the published data doesn't embarrass merchants who have honestly stopped.

4. Lightning-paid coop monitoring as a service

The bet: Productize OpenCluck. Small farm gets a sealed sensor unit, plugs it in, gets Telegram alerts. Subscription paid monthly in Lightning. Hardware sold at cost; recurring revenue is the alert service plus historical dashboard.

Why interesting: Millions of US households keep home flocks; almost none have monitoring. A $15/month Lightning-paid sensor service is differentiated on three axes (Bitcoin native, agent-aware, open-hardware) against zero real competition. Combines the OpenCluck hardware, Bitcoin payment, and agentic operator-service lanes.

First proof step: Deploy two units to friends' coops free for 90 days. See if alerts catch meaningful events and whether they'd have paid.

Biggest risk: Hardware-as-a-service is operationally heavy. Shipping, RMAs, batteries — the support tail can eat a one-person op. Only works if the unit is genuinely plug-and-walk-away.

5. SOUL.md template marketplace for OpenClaw users

The bet: A curated catalog of personality templates (SOUL.md / IDENTITY.md / USER.md) for personal AI agents. Free templates plus a few paid premium ones — "Founder operator," "Researcher," "Solo dev who lives in terminal."

Why interesting: Personal agents are about to become normal, and almost nobody knows how to write a SOUL.md from scratch. Real arbitrage between people who have tuned this for a year (Shane is in that set) and people who just turned on Claude Code last week. Crosses [[agent-personality-design.md]] with [[agentic-business-studio.md]] and produces a public artifact that markets the rest of the studio.

First proof step: Publish three templates as a single GitHub repo under Cash for Clankers — Shane's sanitized SOUL/IDENTITY/USER, a researcher variant, a solo-dev variant. One-page README. See if anyone forks.

Biggest risk: Templates feel uncanny when the user's voice differs from the template's. The real product may be a tutorial on writing your own, with templates as worked examples — not a marketplace. Test framing before committing.

6. Lightning-invoiced WhatsApp reply desk for Los Cobanos operators

The bet: The Whale Window Reply Desk from [[agentic-business-studio.md]], but invoiced and paid in sats weekly via Lightning. No fiat. Combines the El Salvador operator-service lane, the agent-replies lane, and the Bitcoin-payment lane in a country where Bitcoin is legal tender.

Why interesting: El Salvador is the one country where invoicing in sats is not weird. Removes the international wire / PayPal friction that kills most cross-border B2B services in the region. Marketing artifact: "AI reply desk for El Salvador tourism operators, paid in Lightning" is a genuinely novel offer.

First proof step: One pilot operator, one month, fixed sats price. Run the agent-assisted reply flow over WhatsApp Business, document the booking conversion delta, get paid. If it works, the case study is the marketing.

Biggest risk: Bilingual quality. Bad Spanish replies damage a small operator's reputation fast. The agent needs testing against native Salvadoran Spanish (not generic LatAm), with a human review loop fast enough to catch errors before send.

How to use this page

Not a roadmap. The legible side of speculation that would otherwise live as one-off notes. When a bet's first proof step becomes cheap (a lane shipped, a tool got better, the calendar opened), pull it down and convert it to a real project page. When a bet stops being interesting, leave it here as a record of why it didn't survive. The point is the crossover muscle — noticing when two lanes collide.
