Backyard Chickens — Oregon Keeper Notes

Practical reference for keeping 8 egg-laying hens in the Portland/Gresham area on borrowed farmland. This covers what actually matters for a small flock in the Pacific Northwest climate — not a tutorial, a keeper's reference.

Flock overview

  • 8 hens, egg-laying breeds — see [[../entities/the-chickens.md]] for the flock as an entity
  • Location: borrowed farmland in the Gresham area
  • No permanent structure investment (borrowed land constraint)
  • Monitoring: see [[../entities/opencluck.md]] for the IoT sensor project

Egg production by season

Production is strongly seasonal in the Pacific Northwest. Hens respond to daylight hours, not temperature.

| Season | Expected production | Notes | |---|---|---| | Spring (Mar–May) | Peak — close to 1 egg/hen/day | Days lengthening; most breeds hit their annual high | | Summer (Jun–Aug) | High, slight dip in heat waves | Production drops during heat stress above 85°F | | Fall (Sep–Nov) | Declining as days shorten | First-year hens may continue; older hens molt first | | Winter molt (Nov–Feb) | Near zero during molt | Molt lasts 6–12 weeks; hens redirect protein to feather regrowth |

Portland winter reality: The Willamette Valley gets 35–40 inches of rain per year with overcast winters. Day length in December drops to ~8.5 hours of daylight — well below the ~14 hours hens need for sustained production. Artificial lighting (a simple 40W bulb on a timer at dawn) can extend the productive season, but it comes at the cost of shortening a hen's productive lifetime. Without supplemental light, expect 2–3 winter months of near-zero production per year.

Molt timing: Most hens molt in fall. During molt, production drops to zero. High-protein feed (game bird or "feather fixer" crumble) shortens molt duration.

Oregon-specific risks

Wet winters

Oregon's wet season (Oct–Apr) creates persistent coop humidity. High humidity:

  • Encourages respiratory disease (Mycoplasma, aspergillosis)
  • Promotes mold in bedding and feed
  • Raises ammonia faster as wet litter breaks down

Ventilation is the primary mitigation. A coop that breathes — open vents at the peak, not the sides — dries faster than a sealed box. Deep litter (6+ inches of dry pine shavings) composted in place builds heat and manages moisture better than frequent cleanouts in wet weather.

Slugs and mites

  • Slugs: Plentiful in Portland-area gardens, especially fall through spring. Hens will eat them, which is net positive, but slug bait (metaldehyde) is toxic. Iron phosphate bait (Sluggo) is safe around poultry.
  • Northern fowl mites and red mites: Common in the PNW. Red mites hide in coop cracks by day, feed at night. Diatomaceous earth (food-grade) dusted in nesting boxes and coop seams helps. Check hens weekly at the vent feathers — mites cluster there.

Predators

Portland-area predators that take chickens:

  • Raccoons — primary threat; clever, can open simple latches. Use spring-loaded or carabiner-style closures.
  • Opossums — opportunistic, mostly eggs and small pullets
  • Hawks — Red-tailed hawks are resident; Cooper's hawks migrate through. Run cover (overhead netting or dense plantings over the run) matters.
  • Coyotes — present in east Portland and Gresham edge areas
  • Rats — not a direct predator of adults, but take eggs and attract snakes

Hardware cloth (1/2" welded wire) over all openings beats chicken wire, which raccoons can pull apart. Automatic coop doors on a light sensor or timer eliminate the most common error: forgetting to close up at dusk.

Feed, grit, and calcium

Layer feed: 16–18% protein pellet or crumble. All-flock feed at 20% protein is acceptable and simplifies multi-species feeding.

Oyster shell: Free-choice calcium supplement, essential for shell quality. Keep a separate container in the coop at all times. Hens self-regulate intake.

Grit: Hens that forage on grass and bugs need insoluble grit (granite) to grind feed in the gizzard. Free-range birds often pick up enough from the ground; confined hens need it offered.

Scratch grains: Treat only — roughly 10% of diet maximum. High scratch lowers protein ratio in the diet and raises body temperature, which compounds heat stress in summer.

Water: 8 hens drink roughly 1–2 gallons per day in warm weather. Nipple drinkers stay cleaner than open dishes and freeze less readily in winter (though they still need a heated base or daily attention when temps drop below 32°F — which happens maybe 15–25 nights per year in Gresham).

Borrowed land logistics

Borrowed land shapes every decision toward impermanence and low capital exposure:

  • No permanent structures. Coops should be movable or cheap enough to abandon. A used dog kennel with a plywood shelter attached costs $200–400 and can be relocated or sold.
  • No deep improvements to the land. Gravel pads, permanent fencing, or buried hardware cloth all become sunk costs when the land relationship changes.
  • Minimal footprint. A smaller flock is easier to relocate in a crisis than a larger one.
  • Document the arrangement. Even informal borrowed land should have a text message or email trail confirming the arrangement and any shared expectations (who gets eggs, who pays feed, etc.).

The monitoring investment (OpenCluck) is portable — the hardware moves with the coop.

OpenCluck integration

The [[../entities/opencluck.md]] sensor project monitors ammonia, temperature, and humidity and fires Telegram alerts. Key thresholds for Oregon conditions:

  • Ammonia: Alert at 25 ppm. In a closed wet-season coop, ammonia can build quickly if litter management slips.
  • Temperature: Alert below 35°F (hypothermia risk for non-cold-hardy breeds) and above 85°F (heat stress).
  • Humidity: Alert above 70% sustained — indicator that ventilation is failing or litter is too wet.

Egg count tracking is Phase 2 of OpenCluck. A nesting box camera or door-count sensor would let Shane track seasonal production curves without manual daily logging.
