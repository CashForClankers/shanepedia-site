Gresham Vending Ops — Running a Machine in East Portland

The operational layer for Shane's vending machine work in the Portland/Gresham market. The concept and hardware live in Hot Sauce Vending Machine. This page is about running the machine: stocking, location selection, terminal reliability, and the seasonal and logistical realities of east Portland outdoor ops.

Product ops: hot sauce

Hot sauce is one of the cleanest vending products from an operations standpoint:

  • Shelf stable. No refrigeration needed. A properly sealed artisan hot sauce has a shelf life of 12–24 months. Spoilage risk is essentially zero compared to any food cart product.
  • No regulatory complexity. Pre-packaged, shelf-stable food sold from a machine does not require a food handler permit or a commercial kitchen in Oregon. (Verify with Multnomah County Environmental Health before deploying — rules shift.)
  • Small and stackable. A 5 oz bottle fits standard snack machine coils. No custom machine modification required for basic placement.
  • Impulse price point. $10–14 is the sweet spot — high enough to cover margins, low enough that a food-curious person at a cart pod doesn't think twice.

Restock cadence: At 20–30 bottles/week (optimistic for a single machine at one pod), a full machine load (~50–80 bottles depending on coil configuration) lasts 2–4 weeks. Weekly restocking gives a consistent touchpoint with the location operator and lets Shane catch jams and terminal issues before they compound.

Food cart pod selection — east Portland and Gresham

Portland food cart pods vary significantly in foot traffic, clientele, and operator dynamics. Not all pods are equal for a vending deployment.

What makes a pod work:

| Factor | What to look for | |---|---| | Foot traffic | Lunch + dinner + weekend peaks; not just weekday lunch | | Pod size | 8–20 carts is the sweet spot — big enough for real traffic, small enough to matter to the operator | | Audience | Food-curious, willing to try new things; not just fast-lunch workers | | Operator relationship | Single operator or small partnership; easier to negotiate than corporate-managed sites | | Power access | Outdoor outlet or permission to run an extension; needed for the terminal | | Covered or sheltered | Critical for winter; an exposed outdoor pod loses traffic in November–March |

East Portland and Gresham pod landscape:

The food cart pod format is more concentrated in SE and inner east Portland than in Gresham proper. Gresham has individual carts at commercial strips (Powell Blvd, Division) but fewer established pods. For first deployment, target SE Portland pods with established foot traffic:

  • Alberta Arts District / NE Alberta — high food-curious foot traffic, weekend market overlap
  • SE Division Street pods — dense corridor, neighborhood regulars
  • Mississippi Ave area — slightly upscale, good afternoon and weekend traffic
  • Gresham downtown — lower foot traffic than SE Portland, but closer to home base; better for a second machine once ops are dialed in

Avoid: High-turnover pods with frequent cart changes (unstable operator relationship), pods dominated by a single national-brand cart (operator less likely to experiment), and any location without covered seating — Portland weather kills outdoor commerce November through March.

Machine ownership vs. revenue share

Machine ownership (recommended starting point):

  • Shane owns the machine, manages it, keeps the margin
  • Operator gets a flat monthly placement fee ($0–150/month depending on location quality) or a small revenue share (5–15%)
  • Full control over pricing, stocking, and terminal choice
  • Risk: full capital outlay ($500–2,500 for a used snack machine), full responsibility for maintenance

Revenue share / consignment with operator:

  • Operator hosts the machine in exchange for a percentage of gross sales
  • Reduces upfront risk but requires an operator willing to share floor space for a variable payout
  • Harder to find; most pod operators prefer a predictable flat fee
  • Appropriate only if an operator approaches you

Starting recommendation: Own the machine, offer the operator a flat $50–100/month or a 10% rev share, whichever the operator prefers. Keep it simple enough to shake hands on.

Terminal and payment realities

Connectivity:

  • Food cart pods typically have WiFi for the carts, but reliability varies. Do not depend on pod WiFi for payment confirmation.
  • LTE backup via a dedicated hotspot ($20–30/month) is the reliable path. A SIM-based cellular router on the machine eliminates dependence on pod infrastructure.
  • Lightning payment confirmation requires an active internet connection. Card payments via Stripe Terminal can be stored offline for a short window, but LTE is cleaner.

Uptime and failed payments:

  • Machines jam. Coil-type snack machines are the most reliable for non-perishable products but still jam occasionally.
  • A jammed vend after a successful payment is the worst UX failure — the customer paid and got nothing. A simple "product not dispensed" detection (weight sensor at the drop bin or a vend confirmation loop) reduces this. Without a sensor, a printed refund QR or contact number on the machine is the minimum.
  • Card terminal software crashes are real. Stripe Terminal and BTCPay both require reboot procedures. A weekly in-person check catches 90% of issues before they become multi-day outages.

Card terminal choices: The full hardware decision is in Vending Machine Terminal Technology — Lightning + Credit Card. For ops purposes: the terminal must be rated for unattended use (not just POS use) and must be in a weatherproof or indoor-grade enclosure. An outdoor pod in Portland needs at minimum an IP-rated enclosure or a sheltered mount.

Seasonal considerations

Portland outdoor ops are weather-constrained:

| Month range | Conditions | Impact | |---|---|---| | May–Sep | Dry, warm, long evenings | Peak foot traffic at pods; best sales window | | Oct | Starts to rain; cooling | Traffic drops 20–30% at uncovered pods | | Nov–Mar | Wet, cold, overcast | Covered pods survive; exposed pods can lose 50%+ of foot traffic | | Apr | Transition; unpredictable | Improving but volatile; good time to renegotiate placement if needed |

Implications for deployment timing: Launch in spring (April–May) to validate pricing and sales before the wet season. Use the summer to accumulate cash and data. Negotiate a rent reduction or suspension clause for winter months in the placement agreement — operators understand the seasonality.

Machine hardware in the cold: Hot sauce is fine at low temperatures. The payment terminal (Raspberry Pi or tablet) can be affected by cold — keep it inside an insulated enclosure or in a sheltered mount. Stripe Terminal hardware is rated to 32°F operating temperature. Below that is a problem; Portland rarely gets there for sustained periods but it does happen.

Operational cadence

Weekly:

  • Restock (15–30 min per machine)
  • Check terminal connectivity and payment log for errors
  • Wipe the machine face, check for jams
  • Brief check-in with pod operator — keeps the relationship warm

Monthly:

  • Review sales data: top SKUs, payment method split (Lightning vs card), time-of-day peaks
  • Reconcile revenue against stocking costs
  • Check terminal firmware / software updates
  • Assess placement ROI — is this location performing? Is there a better option?

As-needed:

  • Jam clearing (can often be triggered remotely with a vend command if MDB is integrated)
  • Terminal reboot (doable with remote access to the Pi if SSH is set up over LTE)
  • Operator relationship management (lease renewal, placement fee negotiation)

Scaling from one to multiple machines

One machine is the learning vehicle. The ops model scales only when:

  • Unit economics are positive at machine 1 (gross margin after stocking, placement fee, and terminal costs covers Shane's time)
  • Restock and maintenance are under 2 hours/week per machine
  • The operator relationship is durable (not at risk of being pushed out)

At 2–3 machines, a simple route becomes possible: restock all machines in a single 3–4 hour loop per week. East Portland pod density makes this feasible — most target pods are within a 5-mile radius of each other.

Beyond 3 machines, consider the "Rent-a-Human" ops layer from Hot Sauce Vending Machine — paid field execution for stocking and maintenance, freeing Shane to focus on product and terminal decisions rather than logistics.
