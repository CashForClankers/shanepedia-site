The Chickens

Eight hens, all egg-layers, living on borrowed farmland in the Gresham area. The smallest entity in Shanepedia by mass and one of the most consequential by what it teaches. Proof users for OpenCluck, the original reason the IoT project exists, and a quiet anchor for someone who otherwise spends most of his attention on agents and code.

The flock

  • Count: 8
  • All laying hens. No rooster, no meat birds, no pullets in rotation.
  • Location: borrowed farmland in the Portland/Gresham area. Shane does not own the land — see [[../topics/backyard-chickens-oregon.md]] for borrowed-land logistics.
  • Peak output: half a dozen to a full dozen eggs daily depending on season, age, molt cycle.
  • Winter molt output: approximately zero. The Pacific Northwest takes its tax in November.

A mixed laying flock — practical breeds prioritized for reliable production, not show quality. Mixed flocks resist disease pressure better than monocultures, which matters on borrowed land where rebuilding from a die-off is expensive in money and goodwill.

Why eight is the right number

Not arbitrary. The number where the math of a backyard flock works.

  • Enough for friends, family, and neighbors. At peak, eight hens produce more eggs than one household uses. The surplus becomes gifts, neighbor trades, the occasional dozen handed to the right person. The actual ROI is relationships, not revenue.
  • Small enough that care is not a job. Feeding, watering, and locking up is a 10-minute daily task. Cleaning the coop is a weekend hour. Doubling the flock would tip it from "hobby with eggs" into "operation with chores" — the wrong shape.
  • One coop, one run. No second structure, no separate flock dynamics, no integration of new birds.
  • Survives a sick bird. Losing one out of eight is a flock event, not a collapse. Losing one out of three is half the eggs gone overnight.

The Shane Test ([[../topics/the-shane-test.md]]) is implicit — eight hens passes question 5 (survives two weeks away if a friend tops up feed and water) where 80 hens would not.

Connection to OpenCluck

The chickens are why [[opencluck.md]] exists. The IoT project is not a hardware exercise looking for justification — it answers a real problem. A flock on borrowed land is low-margin and cannot absorb preventable die-offs. Ammonia, heat waves, humidity-driven respiratory disease — silent threats an unattended coop can hide until it is too late.

The eight hens are:

  • The Phase 1 deployment target. First unit mounts in their coop, not a test rig.
  • The reason the alert thresholds sit where they do (25 ppm ammonia, 35°F to 85°F band, 70% humidity ceiling).
  • The Phase 2 use case for egg-count vision. Eight birds is the right scale to validate per-hen curves without noise from a hundred-bird flock.
  • The reason Shane has actually held a soldering iron in 2026 instead of just reading about ESP32 boards.

If OpenCluck ever gets productized — see the "Lightning-paid coop monitoring as a service" entry in [[../topics/lateral-bets.md]] — these birds are the first deployed unit and the first real reference customer.

The egg-economy lateral bet

Surplus eggs currently move through informal networks — friends, family, the occasional neighbor at the right moment. The lateral bet (see [[../topics/lateral-bets.md]] entry 1) is whether some fraction could move through a Lightning-paid roadside cooler. Honor box first, then maybe more.

The story would be unbeatable: "I sold an egg my chicken laid for sats." That sentence is the artifact the entire Bitcoin circular economy lane is built to produce.

Honest note: scaling eggs to feed a real machine means more hens, which tips past the right-scale line. The realistic version is one small unit, a few dozen eggs a week, more proof point than business.

Practical care

Detailed keeper notes — feed, grit, calcium, predator defense, seasonal production curves, Oregon-specific risks — live in [[../topics/backyard-chickens-oregon.md]]. Short version: layer feed plus free-choice oyster shell, deep litter through the wet season, hardware cloth over every opening, automatic coop door so dusk lockup never gets forgotten on a busy evening.

Why having animals matters here

Most of Shane's day is agents and code. Telegram messages, Claude sessions, wiki edits, terminal windows. The work is real but it lives entirely on screens, and a life entirely on screens drifts toward abstraction without something tugging it back to ground.

Chickens do not care about your agent stack. They want feed at dawn, water that has not frozen, the coop door closed before the raccoons come out. They impose a daily physical loop no productivity system can argue with.

That ground matters. It keeps the work on the screen honest. The vending machine concept gets sharper when the person designing it has done a real-world stocking run. The OpenCluck product story is credible because the eight proof users are pecking at the dirt thirty feet from where the prototype is being soldered. Eight hens, borrowed farmland, and a quiet daily routine the agents will never replace. That is the right shape.
